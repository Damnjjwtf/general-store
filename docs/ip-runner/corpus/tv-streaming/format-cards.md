# Format deck — ids 201–212

The container and its delivery. Additive to Structure Lab's library; ids chosen not to collide with structure (1–30) or tone (101–120).

Format is load-bearing or it's a runtime. The test: if you swapped the format and the beat-map didn't change, format isn't doing work.

---

### 201 · Four-Act Network Hour
- **mechanism:** ~42 minutes split by four commercial breaks, plus a teaser and often a tag. Each act ends on a turn that must survive three minutes of advertising.
- **engineers:** Compulsion at fixed intervals. The viewer is repeatedly *caught* — attention is re-won four times an hour, so the show can never coast.
- **example:** *The Good Wife*
- **failsWhen:** Act-outs become artificial gasps — a reveal manufactured on schedule rather than earned. The engine starts serving the clock instead of the story.

### 202 · Five-Act Cable Hour
- **mechanism:** An extra break subdivides the middle, giving the second act a hinge instead of a sag.
- **engineers:** Momentum through the traditional dead zone. The viewer never feels the middle-act drift.
- **example:** *Breaking Bad*
- **failsWhen:** The extra turn has nothing to turn on, and act three becomes filler between two hinges.

### 203 · Premium Hour, No Act Breaks
- **mechanism:** Continuous 50–70 minutes, built in scene-sequences rather than acts. Shape comes from escalation and juxtaposition, not interruption.
- **engineers:** Immersion and dread. Nothing releases the viewer, so tension accumulates rather than resetting.
- **example:** *Mad Men*
- **failsWhen:** Without the discipline of act-outs, scenes become atmospheric rather than consequential. "Nothing happens" — beautifully.

### 204 · Streaming Variable-Length
- **mechanism:** Runtime set per episode by story need. A season may run 34, 58, 41, 72 minutes.
- **engineers:** The feeling that the story is telling itself — that shape is organic, not imposed.
- **example:** *The Haunting of Hill House*
- **failsWhen:** "As long as it needs" becomes "as long as it got." No container means no pressure to cut, and the season bloats.

### 205 · Half-Hour Single-Cam Dramedy
- **mechanism:** 22–32 minutes, film-style coverage, no laugh track. Dramatic weight carried in a comic runtime.
- **engineers:** Intimacy and tonal whiplash — the viewer is allowed to laugh and be wounded inside the same scene.
- **example:** *Fleabag*
- **failsWhen:** The runtime can't hold the drama being asked of it, and the show becomes a truncated hour that keeps promising a depth it has no minutes to reach.

### 206 · Multi-Cam Sitcom
- **mechanism:** Three-camera proscenium, live audience, joke-rate architecture. Story serves the setup/punch cadence.
- **engineers:** Communal permission. The audience laugh tells the viewer they are in a room with others.
- **example:** *Cheers*
- **failsWhen:** Sincerity is attempted at joke rate. The form punishes silence, so real feeling has nowhere to sit.

### 207 · Cold Open as Thesis
- **mechanism:** A pre-title unit that states the episode's key in miniature — often through an image or object rather than plot.
- **engineers:** A frame for reading everything after. The viewer is handed the question before the story asks it.
- **example:** *Better Call Saul*
- **failsWhen:** It becomes a decorative prologue with no rhyme to the body — a mood piece the episode never earns back.

### 208 · Tag / Button
- **mechanism:** A short post-climax coda after the last act-out. Comic release, ironic counterpoint, or a quiet hold.
- **engineers:** Aftertaste. The last thing the viewer carries out is chosen deliberately rather than defaulting to the climax.
- **example:** *Parks and Recreation*
- **failsWhen:** Used to dump plot the episode failed to place, or to undercut an ending that should have been left alone.

### 209 · Binge Drop
- **mechanism:** Whole season released at once. Episode boundaries soften; the season reads as one long text.
- **engineers:** Absorption. The viewer loses the week-long gap in which doubt and competing attention live.
- **example:** *Stranger Things* (season drops)
- **failsWhen:** Episodes stop being units. Nothing is designed to be *remembered across a week*, so the show evaporates after the binge.

### 210 · Weekly Release
- **mechanism:** One episode a week. Each must survive seven days of speculation and discourse.
- **engineers:** Anticipation and community. The gap becomes part of the text — theorising is participation.
- **example:** *Severance*
- **failsWhen:** An episode that would pass unnoticed in a binge is exposed as filler; a week of attention finds every seam.

### 211 · Hybrid Drop
- **mechanism:** A batch (2–3 episodes) to establish, then weekly. Or a mid-season split.
- **engineers:** Onboarding without losing the week. The viewer gets past the pilot's cost of entry, then is held.
- **example:** *The Bear* (batch), *Andor* (batched arcs)
- **failsWhen:** The batch boundary lands where no arc closes, and the switch to weekly feels like a rug pull.

### 212 · Limited / Anthology Season
- **mechanism:** Finite, closed season. No renewal obligation; the season is the unit and may reset cast, era, or world.
- **engineers:** Novelistic trust. The viewer invests knowing the story will actually end.
- **example:** *Fargo*
- **failsWhen:** Success forces a second season the structure never planned for — and the show has to retrofit an engine it was designed not to need.
